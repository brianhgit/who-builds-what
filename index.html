1 of 5The idea in 30 seconds

When a company reports earnings, its stock often jumps the next day. We predict how big that jump will be, before it happens. Up or down doesn't matter, only the size: an 8% drop counts as 8%, same as an 8% rise.

What actually happens

Thursday, 4:05 pm
Netflix releases its earnings report, just after the market closes for the day.
Friday
+9%
The stock reacts to the report. Netflix closes up 9%, and the whole market is up 1%.
The real move
8%
The jump caused by the report: 9% minus the market's 1%.

Two guesses, locked in before the report

Both guesses have to be made before Thursday's report comes out, so neither one knows how it went. Ours are locked in the weekend before.

The simple guess
6.8%
Netflix's usual earnings move: the middle of its last 8. See how it's worked out
Our model
7.6%
Starts from the same history, and also looks at how jumpy the stock has been lately and how nervous the market is.
Real move8%
Model missed by0.4
Simple guess missed by1.2

The model was closer, so it wins this one. We do this for thousands of past reports. If the model is closer more often, we beat the baseline. That's the project.

Where does the simple guess come from?
  1. Take Netflix's last 8 earnings moves. Only the size counts, so drops count too.
  2. Line them up from smallest to biggest.
  3. The middle is the simple guess. With 8 moves there are two in the middle, so average them: (6.2 + 7.4) ÷ 2 = 6.8%.

Daniel · Earnings dates

Find exactly when each company announced earnings, and how much its stock usually moves.

What you build

This week you build and test this on fake data with the same columns as the real thing. Tap "Where's the data from?" to see the real source we switch to next week.

  • edgar.pyPull each company's filings from the SEC website and keep only the earnings announcements, with the exact minute each went out.
    Where's the data from?

    The SEC's free EDGAR website, where companies post official filings. You download it.

  • history.pyLook back at each company's last 8 reports and take the middle move. That's the simple guess we're trying to beat.
    Where's the data from?

    The past moves measured in Charvee's table. No download.

Why it matters
A 7 am report counts that day. A 4:30 pm report counts the next day. Wrong time means we measure the wrong jump.

Isani · Prices and live forecasts

Get every stock's daily prices and turn them into "how jumpy has it been lately" numbers.

What you build

This week you build and test this on fake data with the same columns as the real thing. Tap "Where's the data from?" to see the real source we switch to next week.

  • prices.pyDownload daily prices for about 500 companies, plus SPY (the whole market) and the VIX (the market's nervousness gauge).
    Where's the data from?

    Yahoo Finance, through the yfinance Python library. You download it.

  • price.pyUsing prices up to the cutoff day only, measure how much each stock has bounced around lately. Never look past the cutoff.
    Where's the data from?

    The prices you downloaded. No new download.

  • forecast.pySends out our real predictions each week. Goes live in Week 2.
    Where's the data from?

    Philip's model and that week's prices.

  • score.pyGrades those predictions after the reports come out.
    Where's the data from?

    Fresh prices downloaded after the reports come out.

Why it matters
Stocks that have been jumpy lately tend to jump more at earnings. These are the clues the model has that the simple guess doesn't.

Charvee · Dataset

Build the one big table everyone uses: one row per earnings report, with the real move and every allowed clue.

What you build

This week you build and test this on fake data with the same columns as the real thing. Tap "Where's the data from?" to see the real source we switch to next week.

  • calendar.pyWhich days the stock market is open, including holidays and half days, so a move is never measured on a day the market was closed.
    Where's the data from?

    It uses exchange_calendars, a Python library that already knows the New York Stock Exchange's open days, holidays and early closes. No download. You wrap it in simple helpers like "next trading day".

  • windows.pyGiven when a report came out, pick the closing prices just before and just after it.
    Where's the data from?

    The report times in Daniel's filings, plus your calendar.

  • build.pyPut it together: measure each move minus the market's move, attach the clues, and set aside bad rows with a reason.
    Where's the data from?

    Daniel's filings and Isani's prices, saved as files in the project, plus their clue-making code. No download of your own.

Why it matters
The model, the scores and the app all read from this table. A wrong row here is wrong everywhere.

Philip · Model

Build both guesses and test them fairly: train on the past, predict the next quarter, repeat.

What you build

This week you build and test this on fake data with the same columns as the real thing. Tap "Where's the data from?" to see the real source we switch to next week.

  • baseline.pyThe simple guess: the middle of the last 8 moves.
    Where's the data from?

    The history columns in Charvee's table.

  • lgbm.pyThe model: LightGBM, which learns from the 9 clues in the table.
    Where's the data from?

    Charvee's table. No download.

  • shrinkage.pyPulls the model partway back toward the simple guess when that helps.
    Where's the data from?

    Your model and the simple guess.

  • splits.pyThe practice tests, one quarter at a time, 2022 to 2024. Plus predict_folds to run them.
    Where's the data from?

    The report dates in Charvee's table.

Why it matters
A model that peeks at the future looks amazing in testing and fails for real. Your splits keep the test honest.
